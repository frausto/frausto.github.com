I used to measure a good week by how much I had done. More tickets closed, more messages answered, more plates spinning. It took me a long time to notice that motion and progress are not the same thing.

## The cost of everything

Every commitment is a lien against your future attention. Say yes to a project and you have not just added work — you have subtracted focus from everything else you care about.

This is obvious in the abstract and invisible in the moment. The invitation always looks small. It is only in aggregate that you feel the weight.

## Subtraction as a skill

We are taught to add: more skills, more habits, more tools. Almost no one teaches subtraction, and yet the people I admire most are ruthless about it.

- What can I stop doing entirely?
- What am I doing out of habit rather than intent?
- What would I never start today, knowing what I know now?

Cutting things is uncomfortable. It feels like loss. But a garden that is never pruned does not become abundant — it becomes a mess.

## The reward

When you do fewer things, you do them better, and you actually experience them. The work gets more room to breathe. So do you.

Fewer, deeper, slower. It is the least efficient-sounding advice I know, and the most useful.
